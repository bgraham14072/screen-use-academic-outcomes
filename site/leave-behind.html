Bottom line

This collection supports neither a blanket ban nor unlimited devices. Our policy judgment is to favor purposeful, limited, teacher-led use matched to the learning goal, while protecting non-digital learning and attention. This is not a tested comparison of statewide policies.

Five findings to hold together

Instruction can help. Elementary mobile-learning lessons showed a small average benefit, but it became smaller and uncertain after weaker studies were removed.1 Records D1–D2

Television and gaming deserve caution. More television viewing and recreational gaming were linked with lower academic performance, without proving cause and effect.2 Records J1, J6

Classmates’ distraction matters. Students reporting distraction from classmates’ devices scored lower in PISA mathematics.3 Record P3

Broad exposure has mixed findings. One synthesis linked broad exposure with weaker language skills, while another found no clear overall academic link.2,4 Records S5, J5

PISA links limited use with higher scores and heavy leisure use with lower scores. Limited learning and leisure use were positively linked with scores compared with none; OECD also reports that learning outcomes were often better with 1–5 hours of learning use. For leisure at school, students using devices up to 1 hour scored 49 mathematics points higher than those using them 5–7 hours, after socioeconomic adjustment. Scores rose somewhat beyond 7 hours but remained below limited use. These associations establish neither cause and effect nor a recommended dose.5 Records P1–P2, P7

Limits: These are different populations, tasks and study designs, not a contest between devices and books. Category-level findings do not validate every product, and a positive academic result does not establish health, privacy or AI safety.

This dashboard focuses on academic outcomes. Health and wellbeing effects are summarized separately on the Wellbeing Context tab and are not part of the numerical evidence register.

FROM RESEARCH TO POLICY QUESTIONS

Protect learning. Require evidence.

These considerations refer to proposed S10685, listed in the Senate Rules Committee when checked, not enacted law.6 The dashboard’s New York research and policy crosswalk separates bill language, evidence and unresolved questions.

Access and analog rights · §§31–32

The proposal restricts early-grade device access, uses supervised labs or carts in middle grades, and bars required digital homework in PK–8.6 Ask how schools will preserve print, writing and hands-on work while evaluating goal-specific uses and providing genuinely equivalent, accessible alternatives.

Dashboard connection: D1–D2, R1; Paper & Note-Taking; §31–32 crosswalk.

AI and cameras · §33(1)(e)–(g)

The proposal restricts generative AI, conversational AI and hardware cameras on school-issued devices, with an explicit assistive-technology provision in the conversational-AI clause.6 Ask how privacy, consent, human oversight and appropriate research pathways will be addressed without treating emerging tools as proven.

Independent product evidence · §35(2)(h)

The proposal requires provider self-attestation of an independent study showing improved academic performance related to curriculum objectives.6 Ask what counts as independence and adequate evidence, and how requirements apply to product versions, screening and accessibility tools.

Dashboard connection: L1, N4; §35 crosswalk. Category evidence is not product validation.

Exceptions and early identification · §37

The proposal preserves inherent learning-objective uses, IEP/504 uses, other disability-accessibility tools and translation.6 Ask whether universal screening before a child is identified is clearly addressed; instructional added value does not automatically establish legal eligibility.

Before a classroom use, ask

What is the learning goal? What does the technology add beyond a strong non-digital lesson? How will an adult guide and check learning? What talk, play, reading, writing or hands-on work will be protected?
